# WEEK 1 — INTRODUCTION TO ECONOMICS, CONSUMPTION AND SURVEY DATA

## W1L2 — Micro & Macro Economics: The Role of Data

### 1. What is an economic activity?

Any activity that drives the economy is called an **economic activity**.

There are five real-time economic activities that take place simultaneously in an economy:

1. Consumption
2. Production
3. Exchange
4. Distribution
5. Investment

These activities are interrelated. A change in one activity can eventually affect all the others.

**Example:** During COVID, consumption of goods and services was blocked. This affected the exchange and flow of money, which in turn severely affected production.

### 2. Who are the three groups of agents in an economy?

**Producers:** Firms and enterprises.

**Consumers:** Households.

**Government:** The government acts as both a consumer and a producer.

### 3. What are exchange and distribution?

Exchange takes place when firms and enterprises sell goods and services to consumers in return for money or another resource.

Distribution refers to how the resources involved in exchange are distributed. This distribution has implications for investment and production.

Every exchange has an underlying distribution of resources.

### 4. How are the five economic activities interrelated?

Consumers consume goods and services. Producers produce those goods and services. Resources are exchanged between them, and the distribution of those resources determines investment. Investment, in turn, determines production capacity.

Therefore:

Consumption → Exchange → Distribution → Investment → Production capacity.

### 5. What are Microeconomics and Macroeconomics?

**Microeconomics** studies economic activities at the level of individuals, households and firms.

Examples include:
- Consumer purchasing decisions.
- A firm's resource allocation.
- A company's labour productivity.
- The price and demand for a particular product.

**Macroeconomics** studies economic activities at an aggregated level, such as the state, nation, global economy, industries and sectors.

Examples include:
- National consumption trends.
- GDP.
- Industry-level economic activity.
- National income and expenditure.

### 6. What is the role of data analysis at the micro and macro levels?

At the **micro level**, firms collect data about important parameters such as resource allocation, energy consumption and labour productivity. A data analyst studies this data and provides insights that help firms make decisions, improve efficiency and allocate resources effectively.

At the **macro level**, analysts study national-level data to understand the behaviour of economic activities, such as consumption trends. National Sample Survey data is one example of a source of consumption information released by the Central Statistics Office (CSO), as described in the notes.

## W1L3 — Production, Consumption and Exchange

### 1. What is production and value addition?

Production is the process of converting raw materials into usable goods.

The usability or usefulness of a good gives it value. Therefore, production adds value as raw materials are converted into useful goods.

**Example:** Raw materials are converted into a finished product that consumers can use.

### 2. What is utility?

Utility is the usefulness or satisfaction associated with a commodity.

Utility is implicit in a commodity. A commodity without utility value would have no takers.

### 3. How does a producer determine what and how much to produce?

Producers require different kinds of data to make production decisions, including:

- Price data.
- Real-time data.
- Continuous flow of data.
- Resource data.

Analysing these data helps producers answer questions such as:

- Which products should be produced in the coming years?
- Should the firm produce high-value, low-cost products?
- Should it produce low-value, high-cost products?
- How much should it produce?

### 4. How is price a coordinating mechanism?

Price coordinates buying and selling decisions in an economy.

It also acts as a mechanism for determining value and influencing decisions made by buyers and sellers.

### 5. What is consumption and satisfaction?

Consumption means using or consuming a good or service.

The basic unit of consumption is a consumer. A consumer consumes a good or service and derives satisfaction from it.

There would be no reason for production if there were no consumption.

A producer tries to maximise consumer satisfaction by studying consumption data and understanding consumer behaviour.

### 6. What is consumption theory?

Consumers have limited means, such as income, while their wants may be unlimited.

Consumption theory is the formulation or generalisation of standard relationships that explain how consumers tend to behave.

**Example:** As the price of a commodity increases, its demand tends to decrease.

## W1L4 — Consumption Baskets

### 1. What is consumption?

Consumption refers to the goods and services used by a household or an individual.

For example, a household may consume vegetables, electricity, water, internet data and transportation services.

### 2. What is a consumption basket?

When household consumption is categorised over a particular period, such as a day, week or month, it is called a **consumption basket**.

Examples of items in a household's monthly consumption basket include:

- Transportation.
- Ration and groceries.
- Electricity.
- Rent.
- Water.
- Other household expenses.

### 3. Why are consumption baskets important?

A household's monthly consumption basket provides useful information for assessing demand.

If we study the monthly consumption baskets of several thousand households belonging to a particular income bracket, we can identify patterns in consumption.

These patterns help us understand customer behaviour and demand.

### 4. How does household income affect consumption patterns?

Household income forms the upper limit on household spending. This is also referred to as the **household budget**.

The lecture discusses households with monthly incomes of ₹15,000, ₹30,000 and ₹50,000 and compares their consumption patterns.

The main idea is that different income levels can result in different consumption baskets.

## W1L5 — Survey Data

### 1. What is sampling?

Sampling is a technique of selecting individual members or a subset of a population to make statistical inferences about the entire population.

Instead of surveying every individual, researchers select a representative sample.

### 2. What is stratified sampling?

Stratified sampling is a method in which the population is divided into different groups or strata, and sampling is modelled around those groups.

Examples of strata include:

- Income.
- Region.
- Occupation.

The sample should represent the population appropriately.

### 3. What is sample size?

Sample size refers to the number of individuals or observations selected for a survey.

The sampling data and sample size should be representative of the entire population.

### 4. What are the two kinds of biases in consumption survey data?

**A. Bias due to timing of the survey**

If surveys are conducted at different times across different regions, the results may be affected by differences in timing.

The notes explain that surveys should ideally be conducted across the country at the same time to avoid this kind of bias.

**B. Recall bias**

Recall bias occurs when participants are asked to remember their consumption history over a period, such as a week or a month.

Participants may not accurately remember what they consumed, resulting in inaccurate survey information.

### 5. What is high-frequency consumption data?

High-frequency consumption data refers to data relating to a list of commodities that are consumed frequently or on a recurring basis.

After the economic reforms of 1991, India experienced an increase in the choice of goods and services. This increased the unpredictability of consumption patterns.

To address this, the Government of India introduced high-frequency consumption data relating to these goods and services.

### 6. What consumer expenditure trends are discussed?

The lecture discusses:

- Composition of consumer expenditure during 1993–1994.
- Composition of consumer expenditure during 1993–2011.
- Rural and urban expenditure breakdown during 2011–2012.
- The relationship between income and poverty.

## W1L6 — Utility and Prices: An Introduction

### 1. What is utility and how does it affect consumer choice?

Utility refers to the usefulness or satisfaction a consumer derives from a good or service.

Consumers generally try to estimate the utility they will receive from a good or service before purchasing it.

Utility is **subjective**. The same good may provide different levels of utility to different individuals.

**Example:** One person may attach a high utility value to watching a movie on its first day and be willing to pay a premium price. Another person may attach a lower utility value to the same movie.

Utility drives consumer choice.

Every household has a limited income, which acts as an upper limit on its spending. This is called the **budget constraint**.

A household must choose among available goods and services based on their utility and the household's priorities.

### 2. How does price act as a rationing device?

If a good or service were free, consumption could potentially become unlimited.

Price plays an important role in limiting consumption. It acts as a rationing device by keeping the use of a good or service within the limits of available supply.

## W1L7 — Other Sources of Survey Data

### 1. What are the limitations of large-scale national sample surveys?

Large-scale sample surveys from official sources may have a limitation: data may not be available at regular intervals.

This makes it difficult to continuously monitor changes in household behaviour.

### 2. What are CMIE Consumer Pyramids Household Surveys?

CMIE Consumer Pyramids is a product of the Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy Pvt. Ltd.

It conducts waves of surveys on Indian households.

The notes mention that the survey wave during September–December 2021 was the 26th wave and covered 236,000 people.

### 3. What are the advantages of CMIE Consumer Pyramids surveys?

The notes identify three advantages:

1. It is a continuous type of survey, conducted in periodic waves.
2. It has good coverage.
3. The data obtained is longitudinal data.

### 4. What is longitudinal data?

Longitudinal data is data collected from the same households or individuals repeatedly over a period of time.

**Example:** A survey conducted on the same household three times a year for several years produces information that can be studied over time.

This helps researchers understand economic mobility, including:

- Changes in consumption.
- Diversification of consumption baskets.
- Changes in income.
- Changes in the types of goods consumed.
- Which goods may have become inferior over time.

### 5. What are the different types of data?

**A. Time-series data**

Data collected at different points in time. The observations are used to track changes over time.

Example given in the notes: GDP.

**B. Cross-sectional data**

Data that provides a snapshot at a particular point in time.

Example given in the notes: NSS data.

**C. Panel data**

Data pooled from both time-series and cross-sectional data.

### 6. Why is market research obtained from consulting-firm surveys important?

For a firm, surveys can act as:

- Market intelligence.
- Forecasting inputs.
- Inputs for strategic decision-making.
- Sources of demand information.

Firms can study current trends and plan how to position themselves five or ten years into the future.

They can use these insights to adjust their supply and meet future demand.

### 7. Why is data visualisation important?

Visualisation helps people understand information and identify patterns.

Examples of charts mentioned in the notes include:

- Bar graphs.
- Stacked bar charts.
- Scatter plots.
- Pie charts.

These visualisations are important for market research and decision-making.
