W2L1 — Utility: Cardinal vs. Ordinal and Indifference Curves
1. Why is consumer behaviour important?

Consumer behaviour involves consumption decisions that determine overall demand.

The notes discuss two approaches to understanding utility:

Cardinal approach.

Ordinal approach.

2. What is the cardinal approach?

The cardinal approach is a quantitative approach in which numerical values are assigned to utility.

Example: A person derives 100 utils from drinking a cup of coffee.

The notation used for two commodities X and Y is:

[
U_X=f(X)
]

[
U_Y=f(Y)
]

Here:

(U_X) = utility derived from commodity X.

(U_Y) = utility derived from commodity Y.

(P_X) = price of commodity X.

(P_Y) = price of commodity Y.

3. What is the ordinal approach?

The ordinal approach is a qualitative approach in which consumer preferences are ranked rather than assigned numerical utility values.

For example, a consumer may prefer commodity X over commodity Y without assigning exact numerical values to their satisfaction.

4. What is utility and what is its measurement unit?

Utility is the level of satisfaction derived from consuming a good or service.

The measurement unit of utility is called a util.

5. What is Total Utility (TU)?

Total Utility is the overall satisfaction derived from consuming a commodity.

6. What is Marginal Utility (MU)?

Marginal Utility is the additional satisfaction an individual derives from consuming an additional unit of a good or service.

Formula:

[
MU=\frac{\Delta TU}{\Delta Q}
]

Where:

MU = Marginal Utility.

ΔTU = Change in Total Utility.

ΔQ = Change in Quantity consumed.

7. What is the Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility?

The law states that as more and more quantities of a commodity are consumed, the additional satisfaction obtained from each additional unit becomes smaller.

In other words, marginal utility decreases as consumption increases, under the situation described in the notes.

The relationship is:

Total Utility increases with quantity up to a certain point. It reaches a maximum and may decrease with further consumption.

9. What constraints do consumers face?

Consumers face two important constraints:

Limited income.

The price of goods and services.

Consumers try to maximise utility based on their available income and the prices of commodities.

10. What is an indifference curve?

An Indifference Curve (IC) represents combinations of commodities that provide the same level of satisfaction to a consumer.

Example: A consumer may receive the same satisfaction from one idli and two vadas as from two idlis and one vada.

These combinations can be represented on an indifference curve.

11. What do higher and lower indifference curves represent?

A higher indifference curve represents a higher level of satisfaction.

A lower indifference curve represents a lower level of satisfaction.

The notes illustrate a current indifference curve, a lower indifference curve and a higher indifference curve.

12. What are the important properties of indifference curves?

Property 1: Downward sloping

An indifference curve slopes downward. If the quantity of one commodity increases, the quantity of the other commodity in the combination must decrease to maintain the same satisfaction level.

Property 2: Indifference curves do not intersect

Each indifference curve represents a different level of satisfaction. Therefore, the curves do not intersect.

Property 3: Higher curves represent greater satisfaction

The farther an indifference curve is from the origin, the greater the satisfaction represented by it.

A rational consumer aims to move towards a higher indifference curve.

W2L2 — Demand and Supply Curves

1. What is a market?

A market is a group of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service.

Buyers determine the demand for a product.

Sellers determine the supply of a good or service.

2. What is demand?

Demand is the outcome when an individual or group of people is both willing and able to purchase a good or service.

Remember: willingness alone is not enough. The consumer must also be able to purchase the product.

3. What is the Law of Demand?

The Law of Demand states that when the price of a commodity increases, its quantity demanded decreases. When its price decreases, its quantity demanded increases, assuming all other things remain equal.

4. What is a demand curve?

A demand curve is a graphical representation of demand in terms of price and quantity.

5. What is a market demand curve?

A market demand curve is the sum of the demand curves of all individuals in the market.

6. What are left and right shifts of the demand curve?

A demand curve can shift left or right depending on changes in quantity demanded at every price.

Rightward shift: Increase in demand.

Leftward shift: Decrease in demand.

Example: During summer, consumers may be willing to buy more ice cream at the same price. This represents an increase in demand and a rightward shift.

During winter, consumers may be willing to buy less ice cream at the same price. This represents a decrease in demand and a leftward shift.

7. What are the five factors that affect the demand curve?

The notes identify five factors:

Income.

Price of related goods.

Tastes.

Expectations about the future.

Number of buyers.

8. What is the relationship between demand and future expectations?

If individuals expect their incomes to increase in the future, demand may increase.

If individuals expect the price of a commodity to increase in the future, they may purchase more of it now, increasing current demand.

9. What are normal goods?

Normal goods are goods whose demand increases when individuals' income increases.

Examples from the notes:

Branded clothing.

Sports bikes.

Dishwashers.

10. What are inferior goods?

Inferior goods are goods whose demand decreases when individuals' income increases.

Examples from the notes:

Bus travel.

Bicycles.

Supermarket own-brand products.

11. What are substitutes?

Substitutes are pairs of goods where an increase in demand for one good leads to a decrease in demand for the other.

Examples:

Soap and body wash.

Train tickets and plane tickets.

Gel pens and ball pens.

LPG and induction stoves.

12. What are complements?

Complements are pairs of goods where an increase in demand for one good leads to an increase in demand for the other.

Examples:

Pencil and eraser.

Tennis racket and tennis ball.

Cereal and milk.

Electricity and electronic devices.

13. What is market equilibrium?

Market equilibrium is a situation in which demand and supply are balanced.

At a given price level, the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied.

Graphically, market equilibrium is represented by the intersection of the demand curve and the supply curve.

[
Q_d=Q_s
]

14. What are equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity?

Equilibrium price: The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. It is also called the market-clearing price.

Equilibrium quantity: The quantity demanded and supplied at the equilibrium price.

15. What is a surplus?

A surplus occurs when quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded.

[
Surplus=Q_s-Q_d
]

This is excess supply.

A surplus usually creates downward pressure on price. Sellers may reduce prices to bring supply and demand back towards equilibrium.

16. What is a shortage?

A shortage occurs when quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied.

[
Shortage=Q_d-Q_s
]

This is excess demand.

A shortage usually creates upward pressure on price.

17. What is the Law of Supply and Demand?

The price of a good adjusts to balance the quantity supplied and the quantity demanded.

In most markets, shortages and surpluses are temporary because prices adjust towards equilibrium.

W2L3 — Changes in Demand and Elasticity

1. What is elasticity of demand?

Demand depends on different factors and changes when those factors change.

Elasticity helps us understand how much demand responds to a change in a particular factor.

The notes classify elasticity broadly into:

Price elasticity.

Income elasticity.

Cross-price elasticity.

2. What is Price Elasticity of Demand (PEOD/PED)?

Price Elasticity of Demand measures how much quantity demanded responds to a change in the price of a good.

Formula:

[
PED=\left|\frac{%\text{ change in quantity demanded}}{%\text{ change in price}}\right|
]

The absolute value means the result is expressed as a positive magnitude.

Example: If the price increases by 10%, PED measures how much the percentage quantity demanded changes in response.

3. What is elastic demand?

Elastic demand occurs when quantity demanded responds substantially to a change in price.

Example from the notes: Chocolate. If chocolate prices increase, consumers may stop buying it and switch to alternatives such as cookies.

4. What is inelastic demand?

Inelastic demand occurs when quantity demanded responds only slightly to a change in price.

Examples given in the notes:

Necessities.

Prescription medicines.

Tobacco.

Alcohol.

The notes explain that consumption of such products may remain relatively unaffected by price increases.

5. What are the determinants of price elasticity of demand?

The notes identify:

Availability of close substitutes.

Whether the good is a necessity or a luxury.

Goods with close substitutes tend to have more elastic demand.

6. Why are goods with close substitutes more elastic?

Consider Pepsi and Coca-Cola.

Suppose Pepsi and Coca-Cola have the same price, but Pepsi increases its price by 10%.

Consumers may stop buying Pepsi and switch to Coca-Cola.

Since consumers have a close substitute available, the quantity demanded of Pepsi can respond substantially to its price increase.

Therefore, goods with close substitutes tend to have elastic demand.

8. What is Income Elasticity of Demand (IEOD)?

Income Elasticity of Demand measures how much quantity demanded responds to a change in consumer income.

Formula:

[
IEOD=\frac{%\text{ change in quantity demanded}}{%\text{ change in income}}
]

Unlike price elasticity, income elasticity can be positive or negative.

9. What is Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand?

Cross-price elasticity measures how the quantity demanded of one good responds when the price of another good changes.

The notes give the formula in the following wording:

\frac{%\text{ change in quantity demanded of first good}}
{%\text{ change in quantity demanded of second good}}
]

The notes classify goods using the sign of cross-price elasticity:

Substitutes: Positive cross-price elasticity.

Complements: Negative cross-price elasticity.

Note: The formula above reproduces the wording in the uploaded notes. The notes' accompanying explanation describes the relationship in terms of a price change in one good and quantity demanded of another.

10. What is Elasticity of Supply?

Elasticity of supply measures how much quantity supplied responds to a change in price.

Elastic supply: Quantity supplied responds substantially to a price change.

Inelastic supply: Quantity supplied responds only slightly to a price change.

11. What determines price elasticity of supply?

The notes identify the time period as a determinant.

In a longer time horizon, producers may have more flexibility to increase supply.

In a shorter time horizon, supply may be constrained by the available time.

13. What is the Income Effect?

The income effect is the change in a consumer's real purchasing power brought about by a change in the price of a commodity.

Example: A consumer earns ₹15,000 per month and buys meat. If the price of meat decreases, the consumer's purchasing power increases. The consumer can now afford more with the same ₹15,000 income.

14. What is the Substitution Effect?

The substitution effect is the incentive to increase consumption of a good whose price falls when its substitute becomes relatively more expensive.

Example: A consumer buys Pepsi instead of Coca-Cola because Pepsi reduces its price. Coca-Cola becomes relatively more expensive, so the consumer switches to Pepsi.

W2L4 — Production Cost

1. Who are the two broad agents of the market?

Consumer: Demands goods and services through consumption.

Producer: Supplies goods and services through production.

2. What are the objectives of a consumer and a producer?

Consumer: Maximise utility.

Producer or firm: Maximise profits.

3. What is one of the most important variables affecting supply?

The cost of production is an important factor affecting supply.

4. Why is understanding cost important for supply decisions?

Once a firm understands the cost of producing a commodity, it can make important supply decisions.

These include:

What goods to produce.

How much to produce.

Which customer segment to target.

Whether production is economically viable.

5. What is Historical Cost?

Historical cost is the actual cost incurred when purchasing an asset.

Examples:

The cost of land purchased in 2021.

The cost of machinery when a plant was initially established.

6. What is Replacement Cost?

Replacement cost is the price required to replace an asset at current market prices.

Example: A firm purchased machinery when setting up a plant. If it needs to replace the machinery today, the replacement cost may be different from the original purchase price.

7. What is a Depreciation Reserve?

A depreciation reserve is an amount set aside over time to help replace an asset in the future.

Example from the notes:

A firm buys machinery for ₹5 crore. After 15 years, it needs replacement, and the same machinery now costs ₹15 crore.

The firm may set aside ₹1 crore every year in a depreciation reserve. After 15 years, it can use this reserve to purchase new machinery without placing the entire burden on the firm at that time.

8. What is Fixed Cost?

Fixed costs are costs that are unaffected by the company's production output or sales.

Examples:

Interest payable to a bank.

Rent payable.

These costs remain independent of whether the firm's sales increase or decrease, according to the examples in the notes.

9. What is Variable Cost?

Variable costs are costs that increase or decrease with production volume.

Examples include costs associated with producing or transporting additional units.

10. What is Real Cost?

Real cost accounts for the physical quantities of different non-monetary factors involved in production.

Examples from the notes:

Number of nails.

Cubic feet of material.

11. What is Prime Cost?

Prime cost is the direct cost incurred in terms of materials and labour involved in production, excluding fixed cost.

Prime costs help determine the selling cost of a commodity so that the firm can earn a profit.

The quantity of output is determined by the producer.

A producer must identify a range of output where production is cost-efficient.

The notes identify the region around the intersection of the Marginal Cost Curve and Total Cost Curve as important for the firm's production decision.

3. What is a reasonable expansion path of a firm?

A firm may expand its production capacity in stages as demand increases.

For example:

Small factory → Medium factory → Large factory.

The firm expands as demand grows.

4. What are Economies of Scale?

Economies of scale are part of the Long Run Total Average Cost Curve.

As production output increases, Average Total Cost (ATC) decreases.

Reasons may include:

Bulk purchases.

Cost advantages.

Technological innovation.

5. What are Constant Returns to Scale?

Constant returns to scale are the region of the Long Run Average Total Cost curve where output and average cost move in a one-to-one relationship, as described in the notes.

The notes state that many firms operate in this region.

6. What are Diseconomies of Scale?

Diseconomies of scale occur when an increase in production output leads to a more-than-proportionate increase in average total cost.

Example from the notes:

A 2% increase in output may result in a 6% increase in total costs.

W2L6 — Make vs. Buy

1. What is a Make-or-Buy Decision?

A make-or-buy decision occurs when a firm decides whether to:

Produce something internally by investing in additional capital.

Purchase it from an external source or outsource the work.

Buying or outsourcing may help the firm avoid investing in additional capital.

Sunk cost is also an important factor in this decision.

2. What is Sunk Cost?

A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered.

Example from the notes:

A firm enters into an agreement with a marketing agency and pays the fee upfront. If the marketing activity does not produce results, the upfront fee has already been spent and becomes a sunk cost.

3. What is an example of a Make-or-Buy Decision?

The notes give the example of Bata.

Bata decided to outsource footwear production to several local firms rather than manufacture everything itself.

Bata provides specifications and quantities, the local firms manufacture the footwear, and Bata brands the footwear with its logo before selling it to customers.

In this example, Bata chooses buy rather than make.

4. What is the notion of a firm having control over price and quantity?

A firm may have control over:

The price it fixes for a commodity.

The quantity it produces.

Both price and quantity.

According to the notes:

Monopoly market: A firm can control both price and quantity.

Competitive markets: A firm can control only quantity.

W2L7 — Production Decisions

1. What are the two main factors concerning production decisions?

Production decisions are closely connected to:

Price.

Quantity.

Pricing is a function of cost and the quantity produced.

2. What is a Production Function?

A production function gives the maximum level of output that can be produced using different inputs.

It represents the relationship between inputs used in production and the maximum output produced during a given period with a given level of technology.

Formula:

[
Q=f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)
]

Where:

Q = Maximum output.

(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) = Inputs used in production.

3. What are the general inputs for a typical industry?

The notes use the acronym KLEM:

K = Capital.

L = Labour.

E = Energy.

M = Material.

4. What is a Short-Run Production Function?

Consider a production function with two inputs:

[
Q=f(X,Y)
]

Where:

Q = Output.

X = Labour.

Y = Capital.

A short-run production function provides the maximum output for given inputs when at least one production input remains unchanged.

Usually, capital remains unchanged in the short run.

It helps a firm understand its short-term production goals.

5. What is a Long-Run Production Function?

A long-run production function allows the firm to vary all production inputs.

The firm is not restricted to keeping one input fixed.

6. What are the three important terms in short-run production analysis?

A. Total Product (TP)

Total quantity of output produced.

B. Marginal Product (MP)

The change in output resulting from a change in a variable input.

[
MP=\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta X}
]

C. Average Product (AP)

Output produced on average per unit of the variable input.

[
AP_X=\frac{Q}{X}
]

7. How does a firm decide whether to hire an additional worker?

Marginal Product helps estimate the additional output resulting from hiring an additional worker.

8. What are the three stages of short-run production analysis?

The notes identify three stages:

Stage I: MP = AP.

Stage II: MP = 0.

Stage III: Beyond MP = 0.

The notes state that firms prefer to operate in Stage I or Stage II. If they enter Stage III, they need to make decisions to return to Stage I or Stage II.

9. What are IRTS, CRTS and DRTS?

These terms relate to long-run production functions.

They help a firm decide whether to establish a new plant in a new location or continue increasing production at its existing plant.

The notes refer to:

IRTS: Increasing Returns to Scale.

CRTS: Constant Returns to Scale.

DRTS: Decreasing Returns to Scale.

10. What is the Cobb-Douglas Production Function?

The notes describe it as a two-input exponential production function involving capital and labour.

Formula:

[
Q=aL^bK^c
]

Where:

Q = Output.

L = Labour.

K = Capital.

a, b and c = Parameters.

The notes provide these conditions:

If b + c > 1: IRTS.

If b + c = 1: CRTS.

If b + c is less than 1: DRTS.

11. What is Capacity Planning?

Capacity planning is important for capital-intensive firms.

Capital-intensive firms have a major portion of their operating costs tied to investments in machinery, equipment and other capital assets.

Example from the notes:

A cement plant operating at only 30% of its production capacity may be an inefficient use of its large capital investment and could lead to losses.

Good capacity planning requires:

A reasonable forecast of demand.

Effective and transparent communication between production and sales and marketing teams.

12. What are Total Revenue, Average Revenue and Marginal Revenue?

Total Revenue (TR)

[
TR=\text{Total Quantity Produced}\times\text{Price per Quantity}
]

Average Revenue (AR)

[
AR=\frac{TR}{\text{Total Quantity Produced}}
]

Marginal Revenue (MR)

[
MR=\frac{\Delta TR}{\Delta Q}
]

Marginal Revenue represents the additional revenue generated by producing and selling an additional quantity.
