WEEK 3 — PRICING STRATEGIES AND FINANCIAL ANALYSIS

W3L1 — Pricing as a Strategic Variable

1. Why is pricing an important strategic variable?

Pricing helps a firm fulfil its strategic objectives.

The price of a product depends on:

The type of market, such as monopoly or competitive market.

The product category.

The firm's long-term vision for the product.

Whether the firm wants to maximise sales, maximise profits or capture market share.

In competitive markets such as FMCG, a firm may not be able to change prices significantly.

2. What are the objectives of pricing strategies?

Products generally have a life cycle.

A newly launched product may initially experience slow sales. It then enters a growth or diffusion stage, faces competition, reaches maturity and eventually enters a declining phase.

Different pricing strategies may be required at different stages.

The notes list these pricing objectives:

Long-run profits.

Short-run profits or hit-and-run profits.

Increasing sales volume and market share.

Company growth through higher profits.

Matching competitors' prices.

Creating interest and excitement about a product.

Discouraging competitors from cutting prices.

Social, ethical and ideological objectives.

Discouraging new entrants.

3. What factors affect pricing?

The notes identify these factors:

Consumer goals: The primary goals consumers have regarding a product affect its pricing.

Unique Selling Point (USP): Whether a product has a USP compared with competing products affects pricing.

Bundling: A firm may bundle low-value and high-value products or services.

Example: Providing sedan-like features in a hatchback car.

Geographical segmentation: Different cities, districts and income groups may have different prices.

Prices may vary across regions within the same country.

W3L2 — Types of Pricing Strategies

The notes list the following pricing strategies:

Market skimming.

Value pricing.

Loss leader pricing.

Psychological pricing.

Going rate pricing.

Tender pricing.

Price discrimination.

Penetration pricing.

Cost-plus pricing.

Contribution pricing.

Target pricing.

Marginal cost pricing.

Absorption cost pricing.

Destroyer pricing.

1. What is Market Skimming?

Market skimming is a pricing strategy that depends on having information about customer demand, including customers' willingness and ability to buy.

Characteristics:

Low-volume, high-price products.

Often used for new or limited-edition products.

Suitable for the short term or until market competition is established.

Examples from the notes:

Limited-edition bikes.

PlayStation.

Newly launched iPhones.

2. What is Value Pricing?

Value pricing is based on surveys and customer feedback about a firm's products.

The firm categorises products according to customer perception, such as:

Status products.

Premium products.

Exclusive products.

The pricing depends heavily on insights into how consumers perceive the products.

3. What is Loss Leader Pricing?

Loss leader pricing involves selling goods or services at reduced prices to encourage large-volume sales for a certain period.

Examples:

Festival sales.

Supermarket special sales.

A free mobile phone offered with a phone contract, as in the example in the notes.

4. What is Psychological Pricing?

Psychological pricing means setting prices based on how consumers perceive them.

Example:

Selling a product at $9.99 instead of $10.99.

5. What is Going Rate Pricing?

Going rate pricing involves continuously monitoring competitors' prices.

A firm may:

Match competitors' prices.

Reduce its price below competitors' prices.

Increase its price when appropriate based on competitors' prices.

6. What is Tender Pricing?

Tender pricing is described in the notes as a low-cost but high-value delivery model.

It is commonly used for government tenders.

Important characteristics:

Many contracts are awarded through tenders.

Reputation and past track record matter.

Entry barriers may be high.

Tender requirements may include a minimum annual turnover.

Example from the notes: A tender may require annual turnover of at least ₹10 crore.

7. What is Price Discrimination?

Price discrimination means selling the same product or service to different people at different prices.

Example: Airline tickets.

The notes explain that this strategy is possible where resale of the product is not possible.

8. What is Peak-Load Pricing?

Peak-load pricing is a variation of price discrimination.

Prices differ depending on the time of use.

Example: Metro prices during peak hours compared with non-peak hours.

9. What is Cross-Price Subsidisation?

Cross-price subsidisation occurs when a firm charges different customers different prices based on their ability to pay.

The notes give this example:

Customer 1 cannot afford a higher price, so the firm subsidises the service.

Customer 2 can afford a higher price, so the firm charges more to recover the subsidy provided to Customer 1.

Examples mentioned include electricity and different classes of train travel.

10. What is Penetration Pricing?

Penetration pricing is a strategy in which a newly launched product enters the market at a very low price.

The objective is to achieve high-volume sales and penetrate the market.

Example from the notes: Jio Fiber.

Characteristics:

Initially very low prices.

Focus on high sales volume.

Suitable for newly launched products.

Commonly used as a long-term strategy.

11. What is Cost-Plus Pricing?

Cost-plus pricing is a pricing model used to maximise the rate of return for companies.

Formula:

[
CP+MU=SP
]

Where:

CP = Cost Price.

MU = Mark-up.

SP = Selling Price.

The notes also define:

[
CP=FC+VC
]

Where:

FC = Fixed Cost.

VC = Variable Cost.

12. What is Contribution Pricing?

Contribution is the difference between selling price and variable cost.

[
Contribution=Selling\ Price-Variable\ Cost
]

The notes express it as:

[
Contribution=CP+MU-VC
]

Since:

[
CP=FC+VC
]

Therefore:

[
Contribution=FC+MU
]

13. What is Target Pricing?

Target pricing starts with the amount of profit the firm wants to achieve.

The firm varies its mark-up to reach the desired profit.

Formula:

[
Markup=\frac{Profit}{Cost}\times100
]

According to the notes:

When demand is low, the firm reduces the mark-up.

When demand is high, the firm increases the mark-up.

14. What is Marginal Cost Pricing?

Marginal cost is the cost of producing one additional unit or one fewer unit.

Formula:

[
MC=\frac{\Delta Total\ Cost}{\Delta Output}
]

Example from the notes:

[
MC=\frac{€80}{2}=€40
]

15. What is Full Cost Pricing?

Full cost pricing attempts to set a price that covers both fixed costs and variable costs.

16. What is Absorption Cost Pricing?

Absorption cost pricing attempts to set a price that covers variable costs and some of the fixed costs.

The notes mention that this may be used in markets where sunk costs are high.

17. What is Destroyer Pricing?

Destroyer pricing involves undercutting prices to prevent a new entrant from entering the market or to force competitors to exit.

The notes describe it as anti-competitive and illegal.

W3L3 — Analysis of Firm Performance: Key Ratios

1. Why do firms conduct financial analysis?

Financial analysis helps firms:

Identify their strengths and weaknesses.

Monitor important performance indicators.

Compare their performance with competitors.

Understand their past, present and future financial condition.

2. What are Financial Statements?

Financial statements are written records that communicate a company's business activities and financial performance.

The notes identify these types:

A. Balance Sheet

An annual financial record that provides information about the company's financial position.

B. Income Statement

Shows sources of income, such as sales and interest.

C. Cash Flow Statement

Provides information about the liquidity position of the company.

D. Statement of Retained Earnings

A statement relating to the retained earnings of the company.

3. What is Ratio Analysis?

Ratio analysis evaluates a firm's performance by examining relationships between different financial figures.

It helps firms:

Study operational efficiency.

Study operational risk.

Compare performance with past periods.

Compare performance with other firms.

Evaluate current performance.

4. What are the limitations of Ratio Analysis?

Different accounting practices: Accounting practices may differ between firms, making comparisons difficult.

Seasonality: A particular quarter may show unusually high performance because of festivals or seasonal demand.

Therefore, quarterly ratios may need to be compared with annual performance to obtain a more meaningful interpretation.

5. What is the Quick Ratio?

Formula:

[
Quick\ Ratio=\frac{Current\ Assets-Stock\ Inventory}{Current\ Liabilities}
]

The notes provide these interpretations:

1:1 is described as an ideal ratio.

3:1 is described as healthy and indicates substantial current assets compared with liabilities.

0.5:1 is described as unhealthy and may put pressure on the firm.

6. What is the Current Ratio?

Formula:

[
Current\ Ratio=\frac{Current\ Assets}{Current\ Liabilities}
]

The notes describe 1.5:1 as the ideal ratio.

A very high or very low current ratio may be problematic.

Too high: May mean too many current assets are tied up in unproductive assets.

Too low: May indicate that the firm is at risk of being unable to meet its payments.

Examples:

5:1 means ₹5 in assets is available to cover every ₹1 of liabilities.

0.75:1 means ₹0.75 in assets is available to cover every ₹1 of liabilities.

7. What is Earnings Per Share (EPS)?

Formula:

[
EPS=\frac{Profit\ After\ Tax}{Number\ of\ Shares}
]

EPS provides a signal or indication for potential investors.

It helps investors decide whether to invest in a particular firm or stock.

8. What is the Price-Earnings Ratio?

Formula:

[
P/E\ Ratio=\frac{Market\ Price}{Earnings\ Per\ Share}
]

The notes state that higher is better.

9. What is the EBITDA Ratio?

EBITDA means Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization in common financial terminology. The uploaded notes use the wording “Earnings before interest, taxes, and amortization.”

The notes give the formula:

[
EBITDA\ Ratio=\frac{Enterprise\ Value}{EBITDA}
]

It measures the operational performance of the company.

The notes state that higher is better for the company.

10. What is the Dividend Ratio?

The dividend ratio gives an indication of the return on investment relative to the share price.

Formula:

[
Dividend\ Ratio=
\frac{Ordinary\ Share\ Dividend}{Market\ Price}\times100
]

11. What is the Gearing Ratio?

Formula:

[
Gearing\ Ratio=
\frac{Long\ Term\ Loans}{Capital\ Employed}\times100
]

It provides an idea of how much of the capital employed comes from long-term loans.

12. What are Gross Profit and Net Profit?

Gross Profit:

[
Gross\ Profit=Total\ Revenue-Variable\ Cost
]

Net Profit:

[
Net\ Profit=Total\ Revenue-(Variable\ Cost+Fixed\ Cost)
]

13. What are Gross Profit Margin and Net Profit Margin?

Gross Profit Margin:

[
Gross\ Profit\ Margin=
\frac{Gross\ Profit}{Total\ Revenue}\times100
]

Net Profit Margin:

[
Net\ Profit\ Margin=
\frac{Net\ Profit}{Total\ Revenue}\times100
]

14. What is Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)?

Capital employed tells us how much capital has been put to use in an investment.

Formula:

[
ROCE=\frac{Profit}{Capital\ Employed}\times100
]

Example:

A ROCE of 25% means that ₹1 of capital employed generates ₹0.25 in profit for the firm.

ROCE can partly indicate how efficiently a firm uses its capital.

15. What is the Asset Turnover Ratio?

Formula:

[
Asset\ Turnover\ Ratio=
\frac{Sales\ Turnover}{Assets\ Employed}
]

It indicates how much sales turnover is generated relative to the assets employed.

16. What is the Stock Turnover Ratio?

Stock piling up is a major problem in stock-intensive industries such as textiles and cement.

Formula:

[
Stock\ Turnover\ Ratio=
\frac{Cost\ of\ Goods\ Sold}{Stocks}
]

It is expressed as times per year.

The ratio indicates how quickly a company's stock is turned over.

The notes associate a low stock turnover ratio with poor customer satisfaction and a lack of interest in the goods.

17. What are Debtor Days?

Formula:

[
Debtor\ Days=
\frac{Debtors}{Sales\ Turnover}\times365
]

Debtor days indicate how long a business takes to recover its debts.

The notes state that shorter debtor days are better for the firm.

18. What are the key financial insights from the UltraTech Cement example?

The notes discuss UltraTech Cement as an example of a capital-intensive industry.

Important observations:

It is a capital-intensive industry.

It is a low-profit-margin industry.

Its PAT margin was 9.1% in FY 2020.

During the COVID-affected quarters, from Q4 2020 to Q1 2021, revenue dropped significantly.

However, PAT did not fall as much, indicating that the company managed its variable costs effectively.

The notes also explain the distinction between investors:

Equity investors: Purchase shares with the expectation of capital gains and/or dividends.

Debt investors: Lend money to a firm or project sponsor with the expectation of receiving repayment and interest.

Other observations in the notes:

ROCE is an important metric for capital employed.

The industry had a low ROCE of 8.8% for FY 2020.

A ROCE of around 10% was described as not acceptable for an equity investor whose general expectation was 15%, but acceptable for a debt investor.

The company had a high debt-to-equity ratio.

It used cash mainly to retire debt.

Its FY 2020 current ratio was 1.21.

Debtor days were 19 days in FY 2020.

The company had successfully reduced debtor days over time.

This improved operating cash, which was used to repay debt.
